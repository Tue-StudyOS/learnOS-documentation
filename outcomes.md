# Appendix: Interview Findings and Project Outcomes

## 1. Student findings

| Key finding | Evidence from the interviews | Implication for the project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Prioritising material was a recurring difficulty.** | Students used past exams, lecturers’ emphasis, classroom hints and expected question styles to decide what to study. Accounts described difficulty judging which topics deserved limited preparation time. | Ground guidance in the actual course and explain the evidence behind priorities, while keeping uncertainty about future exams visible. |
| **Knowledge gaps could remain hidden until late in preparation.** | Accounts included starting past exams too late, discovering insufficient coverage only in the exam, and repeating a previously encountered mistake. Students wanted a clearer overview of topics and their connections. | Check understanding early, revisit mistakes and connect individual exercises to an overview of the subject. |
| **Students already combined several learning methods.** | Reported practices included handwritten notes, summaries, exercise sheets, flashcards, mind maps, quizzes and past exams. Methods varied with the task: memorisation, conceptual understanding or problem solving. | Support existing habits and different task types rather than assume one learning workflow fits everyone. |
| **AI use varied and usually complemented other methods.** | Some students used AI to discuss uploaded slides, generate questions, summarise content or obtain step-by-step explanations. Others did not actively use it. Paper, conventional resources and peer discussion remained part of the process. | Make AI assistance compatible with students’ materials and independent work; do not assume prior adoption or exclusive use of AI. |
| **Adoption depended on trust, cost and a clear benefit.** | Concerns included paying for specialised tools and uncertainty about whether generated material was relevant to the exam. One concern was that outsourcing the selection of important information might remove a useful part of learning. Another account described difficulty discovering and learning to use available tools. | Make the value and basis of the guidance clear, keep access barriers low and involve students in judging relevance. |
| **Time, study conditions and stress shaped preparation.** | Accounts described inconsistent plans, concentrated study shortly before exams, missing contextual notes, distracting environments and fear of forgetting or failing. Some situations favoured listening over reading or writing. | Allow flexible sessions and formats. Additional content alone cannot resolve organisational and emotional obstacles. |
| **Human interaction provided distinctive support.** | Students valued studying with peers who could explain concepts and recognise where a question or misunderstanding came from. | Complement peer learning and discussion, and preserve opportunities for students to explain their reasoning to others. |

The clustering board reinforces these themes through its groupings around prioritisation, unclear knowledge gaps, varied tools, physical study practices, environmental constraints and inconsistent preparation. It is a synthesis of the interviews, not an independent dataset.

## 2. Teaching and educational-project findings

| Key finding | Evidence from the conversations | Implication for the project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Completed exercises could conceal limited understanding.** | Teaching-side accounts described apparently correct AI-assisted submissions that revealed little about independent reasoning. This also made it harder to identify useful topics for tutorials. Concerns about completion-based incentives prompted discussion of alternative assessment practices. | Focus feedback on reasoning and misconceptions rather than treating correct submissions as sufficient evidence of learning. |
| **Exercise incentives involved a trade-off between participation and independent effort.** | A lecturer reported that making exercises entirely optional reduced participation, while AI-solvable submissions weakened the value of completion-based incentives. The conversation highlighted the difficulty of encouraging regular practice without rewarding work that students had not meaningfully engaged with. | Support regular, active practice and distinguish task completion from demonstrated understanding. Feedback tools alone cannot resolve the underlying incentive problem. |
| **Checking independent performance could increase assessment pressure.** | The lecturer discussed presentations of solutions, short quizzes and supervised exercise sessions as ways to make students’ own abilities more visible. These approaches were also described as potentially stressful and only a partial response to the challenges posed by AI-assisted work. | Use chat analysis as supplementary evidence for teaching support, alongside opportunities to observe independent reasoning. Consider the burden of additional checks on students and teaching staff. |
| **Existing feedback practices differed substantially.** | One account relied on classroom reactions and questions, which tended to reveal the students already following well. Another described established feedback surveys and their use when revising teaching. | Complement existing feedback channels and recognise that visible participation may leave some difficulties unobserved. |
| **Feedback needed to support a feasible teaching response.** | Conversations identified interest in recurring problems with particular exercises and common questions in chats. They also highlighted that substantial changes to a lecture sequence might only be feasible in a later iteration. | Provide concise, specific summaries that can inform a tutorial now or a course revision later. |
| **Motivation and independent thinking remained central.** | Teaching-side discussions emphasised curiosity, conceptual depth and human interaction. Concerns included postponing learning or mistaking access to an explanation for independent understanding. One lecturer described an approach that allowed AI use and included demonstrations of its capabilities, while stressing that students should continue investigating rather than simply submit generated answers. | Encourage students to attempt, explain and check their work, and to critically examine AI-generated solutions. Treat concerns about learning effects as interview perspectives rather than effects demonstrated by our research. |
| **Integration and maintenance were conditions for lasting use.** | Discussions covered existing platforms, available data and integration needs. They also questioned whether student-built software would survive after its original developers left. | Address a specific need within existing workflows and define who will integrate, operate and maintain the contribution. |

## 3. Outcomes for the project

The interviews helped refine the initial idea of generating personalised exercises into two connected needs: helping students identify relevant material and gaps in understanding, and helping teaching staff recognise recurring learning difficulties.

We have the following responses to those needs:

1. We prioritised reusable learning skills that work with students’ own materials, support topic orientation and check understanding during practice.
2. We chose to work within familiar AI environments, with support for mathematical notation and images of handwritten work, reflecting the continued importance of paper and varied learning methods.
3. We developed a separate analysis package intended to summarise difficulties and misconceptions in student interactions and suggest possible teaching responses. 
4. We pursued a portable component that partners could incorporate into their systems, making integration and continued ownership part of the project’s scope.
